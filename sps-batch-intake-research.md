### Body Draft Material

A former Garfield High School student received a $16 million settlement from Seattle Public Schools in October 2024 — the largest tort claim settlement in district history — after filing suit in July 2024 alleging years of sexual abuse by two coaches affiliated with the school's athletic program. The plaintiff, who was 24 at the time of settlement, alleged that volunteer weightlifting coach Walter Junior Jones first raped her in 2013 when she was 13 years old and practicing with the Garfield girls' basketball team. Jones reportedly threatened to kill her and her family if she disclosed the abuse, which continued over multiple years. District records show Jones had previously been dismissed from Ballard High School and placed on SPS's "Do Not Re-Hire" list before being allowed to volunteer at Garfield. Garfield Athletic Director Ed Haskins told investigators he was uncertain but "thought he recalled an issue coming up regarding Jones' background check which prevented him from being vetted as a volunteer" — yet Jones remained on campus.

A second coach, Marvin Wayne Hall, who held roles as assistant boys' basketball coach, security staff member, and eventually head coach of the Garfield girls' basketball team, allegedly initiated a sexual relationship with the plaintiff when she was 17, including hotel encounters during team travel. That relationship continued into her early 20s. Hall was fired in 2022 after a separate student reported he had told her he had a "crush" on her. Hall pleaded guilty to sexual misconduct with a minor in summer 2025. Jones faces criminal charges of two counts of felony child rape in King County Superior Court; a jury deadlocked in December 2025 and a mistrial was declared. Retrial status is unresolved as of this research date.

Seattle Public Schools will pay $500,000 of the total settlement, covering its share of settlement fees and defense costs. The Washington Schools Risk Management Pool, the district's insurer, pays the remaining approximately $15.5 million. The district denied liability in the settlement but acknowledged the seriousness of the allegations and stated it would strengthen safeguards. Plaintiff attorneys Paul Sewell and Tomás Gahan of PWRFL Law stated the settlement was the largest injury settlement in SPS history. The lawsuit alleged SPS was negligent in failing to protect the student from both coaches, failed to report abuse, engaged in "extreme and outrageous conduct," and discriminated based on disability.

The case illustrates a documented pattern of institutional failure: a coach with a flagged background remained in contact with students; a second coach used his official access to pursue an exploitative relationship over years; a separate student's complaint about Hall in 2022 finally triggered his removal, years after the abuse of the plaintiff allegedly began. The criminal proceedings remain open, with Jones's retrial status unknown. The district's $8.8 million annual liability insurance premium to WSRMP (as of 2024 reporting) reflects the cost burden of ongoing student safety failures.

### Body Draft Material

A former Garfield High School student with autism received a $125,000 settlement from Seattle Public Schools in May 2024, resolving a civil lawsuit (King County Superior Court, Case No. 22-2-10907-6 SEA) alleging First Amendment retaliation after administrators silenced him for trying to report hazing on the school's varsity swim team. Alex Thornewell, 21 at the time of settlement, said he first witnessed hazing as a freshman in 2017: upperclassmen would squat naked over the faces of freshmen during team activities. He was told, per his account, that "whatever occurs in the locker room remains in the locker room."

For several years Thornewell did not speak publicly about the incidents. In 2020, during his junior year, he agreed to be interviewed by a reporter for the school newspaper. Before a follow-up interview could take place, Thornewell alleges the school's vice principal confronted him, pulled him into a room, and demanded he stop speaking with the reporter. When the school opened a formal hazing investigation — the 2019-2020 school year investigation initiated by Athletic Director Carole Lynch, confirmed in subsequent Public Records Act litigation — the investigator allegedly asked witnesses whether Thornewell's autism made him prone to "exaggerate or fabricate details," a line of questioning his family said was designed to discredit him rather than investigate the underlying hazing.

Thornewell filed a lawsuit in 2022, and a settlement of $125,000 was reached by May 2024. Seattle Public Schools did not admit wrongdoing. His attorneys — Jeff Admon of Admon Law Firm and Lara Hruska and Luke Hackenberg of Cedar Law — described the case as about holding officials accountable, protecting free speech, and defending a vulnerable young person whose disability was weaponized against him during the investigation. A parallel public records dispute (Amanda Thornewell v. Seattle School District No. 1, Washington Court of Appeals Case No. 85998-6-I, decided November 25, 2024) confirmed details of the OSCR investigation timeline and district handling of records, though the Court of Appeals affirmed the district had not violated the Public Records Act.

The case documents a sequence in which a student who witnessed misconduct and attempted to speak to a school journalist was administratively silenced, then had his disability invoked to undermine his credibility during the district's own investigation. The $125,000 settlement, while smaller than the sex-abuse case, reflects a First Amendment retaliation claim that the district did not contest to judgment.

### Body Draft Material

On April 15, 2025, Washington State Attorney General Nick Brown filed a civil rights lawsuit in King County Superior Court against Seattle Public Schools, alleging the district systematically failed to provide legally required accommodations to pregnant and nursing employees across multiple schools for at least four years. The suit was brought by the AG's office under its enforcement authority over RCW 43.10.005 (the Healthy Starts Act) and RCW 49.60.030 and 49.60.180 (the Washington Law Against Discrimination). Brown's office investigated employee complaints and found that SPS "routinely failed to provide legally required accommodations" — and that the district had no district-level policy at all for handling such requests. The case is handled by Assistant Attorney General Diane Lopez, investigator Jennifer Sievert, and paralegal Panda Halford.

The complaint describes a range of failures documented across schools since at least 2021. Employees were denied flexible restroom breaks, modified work schedules, and the ability to sit during shifts — accommodations that are mandatory under state law and may not be denied on undue hardship grounds for certain categories. One employee who was eight months pregnant was unable to sit for an entire workday. Nursing employees were provided no clean, private spaces to express milk, were walked in on while pumping, and developed painful clogged ducts and infections including mastitis. One employee said they "had no choice but to take leave to continue breastfeeding."

The AG's complaint also alleges that employees who sought accommodations faced retaliation: negative performance reviews, formal admonishments for attending medical appointments, removal from preferred classroom assignments, and in some cases periods of unpaid leave or loss of benefits during or immediately after pregnancy. The AG's office says it raised these concerns directly with SPS before filing and sought to resolve the matter out of court; those discussions were "unsuccessful." The lawsuit seeks a court order halting all discriminatory practices immediately and restitution for every affected employee.

Following the lawsuit, SPS published a new district policy — Policy 5010SP.B, Workplace Accommodations for Pregnancy and Nursing — in November 2025, formally codifying accommodation obligations under the Healthy Starts Act and WLAD. Whether this corrective action is sufficient to resolve the lawsuit has not been publicly determined as of this research date. Attorney General Nick Brown succeeded Bob Ferguson in office on January 15, 2025, and this SPS suit was among the first civil rights enforcement actions his office took.
