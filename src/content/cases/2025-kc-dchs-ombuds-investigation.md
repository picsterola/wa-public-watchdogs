**King County's auditor flagged 19 contractors for financial irregularities inside the county's Department of Community and Human Services (DCHS) — and when the agency's own response raised concerns, the county auditor wrote a letter expressing "deep concern" and the investigation was handed to the independent Ombudsman.**

## What happened

Following the King County Auditor's 2025 review of DCHS contracting, 19 contractor organizations were flagged for financial irregularities described as "dubious expenses."

In October 2025, County Auditor Kymber Waltmunson sent a letter to the office of King County Executive Shannon Braddock expressing "deep concern" about how DCHS was handling the audit findings internally. By late 2025, the King County Ombudsman's Office had taken over the investigation from DCHS's internal review process. Ombudsman director Jeremy Bell is overseeing the inquiry, with an outside investigator contract pending.

Examples of irregularities cited in the investigation and Seattle Times reporting:
- An executive director who loaned herself $14,000 without board consent
- A nonprofit executive paid 46% above budget
- Suspected forged invoices and agreements
- A $111,000 nonprofit donation to an organization connected to a board member

## What the primary source says

The King County Ombuds Office report OMB20250697 documents the scope of the programmatic contract evaluation. Seattle Times reporting on November 28, 2025, confirmed the transfer of investigative authority to the Ombudsman and cited specific irregularity examples from the ongoing investigation.

## Status

Updated October 20, 2026.

**The forensic review.** The Ombuds released Clark Nuber's review on May 19, 2026. It found $690,617 in questioned costs across 224 transactions at 16 of the 19 community partners, four instances of potentially altered documents, and six potential conflicts of interest, including a consultant who, according to DCHS, was a relative of the DCHS program manager overseeing the contracts. Clark Nuber put the amount potentially paid improperly or not verified as recovered at $446,703. DCHS asked the Ombuds to change that to $320,000, calling it a "preliminary maximum exposure amount," and the Ombuds declined ([Ombuds report and responses](https://cdn.kingcounty.gov/-/media/king-county/independent/governance-and-leadership/government-oversight/office-of-the-ombuds/reports/dchs-programmatic-contract-evaluation-omb20250697.pdf?rev=fe8aae2694564a26b80b28ee23a160e4&hash=47C130F3291B4CA772BF9A75A411B337)). The review did not assess program outcomes, and it says total exposure "could be materially greater."

**DCHS's final review.** On September 25, 2026, DCHS said its final review found "$10,564 in potential fraud from two of the 19 organizations," that it disallowed those costs and did not pay them, and that the Ombuds referred both cases to law enforcement ([DCHS via King County Employee News](https://kcemployees.com/2026/09/25/final-review-of-audited-contracts-shows-no-loss-of-funds-to-fraud/)). That is the department's own conclusion about its own contracts. The registry has not found an independent review confirming it, and law-enforcement outcomes have not been reported.

**The structural response.** The County Council passed Ordinance 2026-0101 on July 7, 2026 (enacted as Ordinance 20101). It creates an Inspector General Division in the Office of Public Complaints with subpoena power, a fraud hotline, and authority to pursue recovery of misspent funds ([King County Council](https://mkcclegisearch.kingcounty.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=7991470&GUID=4F5FD59F-DB76-4B00-A0AE-9B133F28ED01&Options=Advanced&Search=)). On October 6, the Council appointed Stuart Robinson, a former FBI special agent, as the county's first inspector general for a five-year term ([King County Council](https://kingcounty.gov/en/dept/council/governance-leadership/county-council/newsroom/2026/10-06-council-inspector-general-appointed)). The office is still being staffed.

The detailed forensic record is in [KC-2026-008](/cases/kc-ombuds-dchs-forensic-clark-nuber). No criminal charges have been reported.

## Why it's in the registry

This case documents what happens when an agency's internal review of its own contractors is found inadequate by the independent auditor. The auditor had to write a public letter of concern before the investigation was escalated to an outside body. That escalation path — taking months — is itself a structural finding. This case is downstream from KC-2025-001 (the original DCHS systemic audit).

## Reform implication

The multi-step escalation process here — audit, inadequate internal response, public letter of concern, Ombudsman takeover — is what happens when there is no standing independent investigative authority with direct jurisdiction. An Inspector General reporting to the County Council, not the Executive, would have assumed jurisdiction immediately.
