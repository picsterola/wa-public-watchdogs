legal_status: audit_finding
outcome_summary: |
  Report published August 20, 2025 as the third annual edition of OIG's claims and lawsuits review, mandated by Ordinance 125315 §3.29270(14).

  Coverage: 1,020 claims filed and 1,026 claims closed during 2019-2023; 172 lawsuits filed during 2014-2023; 22 OPA investigations tied to settled claims/lawsuits of $20,000 or more.

  Aggregate financials: $13,042,400 in settlements paid 2021-2023, exceeding the seven-year prior total of $6,005,577.40. Police-action settlements: $10.7M decade total. Excessive-force settlements (2014-2022): $9.38M, representing 90.32% of all police-action settlement dollars. Fleet-negligence settlements: $4.7M. The City carries $20M in excess liability insurance with a $10M self-insurance retention through the Judgment and Claims Fund.

  Discipline outcomes on settled cases: 9.1% of OPA allegations sustained across the 22 high-value investigations OIG reviewed. Discipline where sustained ranged from one-day to six-day suspensions, with two terminations across the entire decade. The most severe disciplinary action tied to the largest single settlement ($3.5M excessive force, 2018 shooting) was a two-day suspension for "equipment and uniform."

  Early Intervention System status: paused. 47 alerts and 11 assessment plans across 2016-2023; zero assessment plans in 2023. SPD developing replacement; no timeline published.

  Collision data: 78% of SPD officer collisions reviewed by the Collision Review Board (CRB) were found preventable.

  No formal recommendations were issued. OIG committed to continued annual reporting and to a forthcoming "comprehensive assessment of SPD officers involved in collisions."

# === RESOLUTION TRACKING ===
remediation_status: not_started
remediation_note: |
  The report explicitly avoids prescribing remediation. OIG's "Conclusions and Next Steps" section commits only to continued annual reporting of claim and lawsuit counts and to "advancing quantitative and qualitative analyses of litigation data."

  Two structural problems the report documents but does not propose to fix:

  First, the Early Intervention System (EIS) — the mechanism intended to flag officers whose force, collision, complaint, and litigation patterns suggest risk — generated only 47 alerts and 11 assessment plans across the entire 2016-2023 review period. EIS is currently paused as SPD develops a replacement system. No timeline for restoration is given.

  Second, the OPA accountability loop on settled cases is weak. Of 22 OPA investigations OIG identified tied to claims or lawsuits settled at $20,000 or more, only 9.1% of allegations were sustained. Where sustained, discipline ranged from one-day suspensions to two terminations, with no clear correlation between settlement size and disciplinary severity. A $3.5M excessive-force settlement resulted in a two-day suspension for an equipment-and-uniform violation.

  OIG's stated next step is "a comprehensive assessment of SPD officers involved in collisions" — narrower than the structural questions the data raises.

# === REFORM ARGUMENT ===
reform_argument: |
  This is the kind of report that should embarrass a city but rarely does. OIG sat on ten years of SPD litigation data and what it shows is a system designed to pay out, not to learn.

  Three findings carry weight beyond the dollar totals.

  One — accountability decouples from cost. Of 22 OPA investigations tied to settlements of $20,000 or more, only 9.1% of allegations were sustained. The single largest excessive-force settlement in the dataset, $3.5 million for a 2018 shooting, produced a two-day suspension for an equipment-and-uniform violation. If the City pays $3.5M to settle a case, and OPA finds the underlying force was not sustainable as misconduct, one of those two judgments is structurally broken. The report does not say which.

  Two — the Early Intervention System is functionally absent. Between 2016 and 2023, EIS generated 47 alerts and 11 assessment plans across an entire department. In 2023 it produced zero assessment plans. The system is currently paused while SPD builds a replacement, with no published timeline. An EIS that flags individual officers based on force, collisions, complaints, and litigation patterns is exactly the early-warning infrastructure the Consent Decree required and the Accountability Ordinance contemplates. Its de facto suspension during the final months of federal oversight is not a small thing.

  Three — settlement velocity is accelerating. The City paid more in SPD lawsuit settlements during 2021-2023 ($13.04M) than in the prior seven years combined ($6.01M). Some of that reflects 2020 protest litigation working through the courts. Most of it does not. The report flags the trend but offers no causal analysis.

  The OIG report is descriptive by design and OIG cannot compel SPD to change anything. The reform implication is upstream: the value of an Inspector General regime depends on whether anyone (City Council, Mayor, or successor federal oversight body) acts on the findings. So far, the legislative response has been silence. The federal Consent Decree lifted in September 2025. The replacement EIS has no published delivery date. The accountability gap this report documents is precisely what local-only oversight is now expected to close.
