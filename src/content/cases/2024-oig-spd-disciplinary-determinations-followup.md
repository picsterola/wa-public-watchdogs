# === STATUS ===
legal_status: audit_finding
outcome_summary: |
  Follow-up audit published December 16, 2024, two days before Mayor Bruce Harrell fired former Chief Adrian Diaz for misconduct. The audit reviewed disciplinary determinations made between 2020 and mid-2024 under Chiefs Best and Diaz, sampling 94 cases where the Disciplinary Committee proposed a discipline range to the Chief.

  Headline findings:
  - Chief's final discipline was at or below the minimum of the proposed range in more than 50% of the 94 cases, reaching 69% in some sampled years
  - In 7 cases the Chief imposed discipline below the recommended range entirely
  - In 46 cases where the recommended range was between reprimand and suspension, the Chief imposed reprimand 70% of the time
  - In 36 cases where only a mitigating factor was identified, 33 (92%) of disciplinary determinations were at or below the bottom of the range
  - For active employees, 65% of cases included weighing of mitigating factors vs 27% for aggravating factors
  - Top mitigating factors cited: Good Record (27), First Violation (23), Length of Service (20), Admission of Fault (18), Chain of Command Support (13)

  Director Certification Memo (DCM) timing has deteriorated. The DCM is increasingly delivered the day of or day before the Discipline Committee meeting, sometimes giving the Committee less than 24 hours to research and adjudicate cases. OPA caseload does not explain the compression — 2022-2023 caseload was lower than 2020-2021.

  Arbitration backlog: 106 disciplinary appeals pending arbitrator selection as of June 2024, with 82 of those carried forward unresolved from the 2021 audit. More than half (70 of 106) concern reprimands or sustained findings without discipline. Of arbitration appeals that did resolve in the audit window: 3 had discipline upheld, 2 had discipline reduced, 2 settled with reductions, 5 were dismissed or withdrawn.

  Only one formal recommendation: that OPA and SPD's Labor and Employment Advisor jointly establish documented timeline expectations for DCM submission, codified in the OPA manual. City Council concurred; implementation estimated Q1-Q2 2025.

# === RESOLUTION TRACKING ===
remediation_status: in_progress
remediation_note: |
  Recommendation 1 (DCM timeline documentation) was concurred-with by City Council with implementation estimated Q1-Q2 2025. No published verification confirms the OPA manual has been updated. Status of implementation as of last verification: unconfirmed.

  Structural problems the audit identifies but does not recommend fixing:

  First, the pattern of below-range discipline. The audit treats the Chief's discretion as legitimate and frames mitigating-factor weighting as descriptive rather than problematic. OIG explicitly states it "did not assess whether aggravating or mitigating factors were valid, or whether additional factors should have been considered." The follow-up to the 2021 audit thus confirms the pattern persists across two chiefs (Best and Diaz) but offers no proposed corrective.

  Second, the arbitration backlog. 82 cases carrying forward unresolved from a 2021 audit is itself evidence that the 2017 Accountability Ordinance's vision of PSCSC as "sole route of appeal" has not been operationalized. OIG notes the SPOG arbitrator-selection controls remain weak. The audit makes no recommendation on this front, noting only that further analysis is needed once the backlog clears — a backlog that is in fact growing.

  Third, the Loudermill meeting dynamic. The audit notes that the Chief's discipline pattern "appears appropriate relative to the volume of mitigating factors cited in DARs but illustrates the impact of Loudermill meetings" — the closed sessions where officers present their case directly to the Chief. The structural question of whether Loudermill meetings systematically inflate mitigating-factor weighting is raised but not pursued.

  This audit is therefore best read as a finding that the 2021 audit's core recommendations were not implemented. Of the 2021 audit recommendations, OIG separately reports that DAR Reconciliation (recommending SPD audit and rectify disciplinary documentation back to 2018) was implemented in early 2025, with SPD identifying and properly filing 379 missing DARs. The pattern of below-minimum discipline has not been addressed.

# === REFORM ARGUMENT ===
reform_argument: |
  This audit is a confession dressed as a follow-up. The 2021 audit told the City that SPD chiefs were systematically pulling discipline below recommended ranges and that mitigating-factor citations outpaced aggravating-factor citations by 140%. The 2024 follow-up confirms the pattern not only persisted but in some years got worse: 69% of cases ending at or below the minimum range, 92% in the subset where only a mitigating factor was identified.

  The political timing matters. Mayor Harrell fired Adrian Diaz on December 18, 2024, two days after this audit dropped, for unrelated misconduct involving a chief-of-staff affair. The disciplinary determinations story got buried under the firing. That is convenient for the City, because the audit's actual finding is structural: two consecutive chiefs (Best then Diaz) ran the same pattern, which means the next chief will face the same incentives unless something in the underlying process changes.

  OIG could have recommended a great deal here and chose not to. The audit explicitly states it "did not assess whether aggravating or mitigating factors were valid." It does not propose a written rubric. It does not propose limits on Loudermill mitigating-factor introduction. It does not propose external review of below-range determinations. It does not propose anything about the 106 pending arbitration appeals, 82 of which have been pending since the 2021 audit. The one recommendation it does make — document the DCM timeline expectation in the OPA manual — is procedural housekeeping.

  The reform implication is twofold.

  First, the Inspector General regime needs sharper teeth. OIG can document patterns but cannot compel structural change. The 2017 Accountability Ordinance's design — PSCSC as primary appellate body — has not been operationalized in seven years, and OIG's audit notes this without prescribing a fix. That is the structural-failure pattern that consent-decree-era reforms were meant to break.

  Second, the pattern this audit describes is exactly the upstream mechanism behind the litigation totals in the 2025 OIG Claims and Lawsuits Report. When 9.1% of OPA allegations are sustained on cases that produced $13M in settlements, and the Chief's discipline on sustained cases trends to the minimum, the City is paying tort settlements for conduct that produces minimal officer-level accountability. That is the accountability gap the federal Consent Decree was supposed to close. The Consent Decree lifted in September 2025. The gap is documented and unaddressed.
