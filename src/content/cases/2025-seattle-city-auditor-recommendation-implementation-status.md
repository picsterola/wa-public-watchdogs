# === STATUS ===
legal_status: audit_finding
outcome_summary: |
  Annual status report published May 19, 2025 by Seattle City Auditor David G. Jones, tracking implementation of 928 audit recommendations issued since January 2007.

  Top-line: 20% of all recommendations since 2007 (182 of 928) have been closed as "no further follow-up planned." Per the City Auditor's own definition, closure occurs when (1) the recommendation is no longer relevant, (2) implementation is not feasible, (3) the audited entity does not agree and will not implement, or (4) City Council considered but did not adopt.

  For the 2024 working cohort of 123 recommendations, only 21 (17%) were implemented during the year while 28 (23%) were closed without implementation.

  Persistent SPD recommendations from prior years that remain open under the Office of City Auditor's continued follow-up (performance audits of SPD have since shifted to OIG jurisdiction, but the City Auditor continues tracking pre-transfer recommendations):

  - March 2015 SPD public disclosure audit: 1 recommendation open (staffing model for public records workload)
  - April 2016 SPD overtime audit (requested by Chief Kathleen O'Toole): 7 recommendations open, awaiting Telestaff/UKG software procurement
  - December 2017 SPD special events staffing and cost recovery audit (mandated by Ordinance 124860): 10 recommendations open, awaiting policy decisions and software implementation

  Other notable status entries:
  - October 2023 SDCI Construction Permitting audit: all 11 recommendations pending
  - December 2023 RRIO (rental registration) audit: 1 of 9 implemented; 8 pending
  - September 2020 SDOT bridge maintenance audit: 8 of 10 implemented
  - 2023 Organized Retail Crime audit: 4 of 10 implemented
  - May 2019 Seattle City Employees' Retirement System (SCERS) funding: pending (44.12% funded vs 24% contribution rate; will be considered implemented only when fully funded by 2042)
  - July 2024 Overdose/crime concentration audit (requested by Council President Juarez): Mayor's Office implemented 2 of 8 recommendations

  Surveillance technology reclassification: 26 recommendations across three audits (Seattle Fire Department CAD/cameras and Seattle City Light diversion tech) were closed in 2024 after the Chief Technology Officer reclassified the technologies as non-surveillance, voiding the underlying audit basis.

# === RESOLUTION TRACKING ===
remediation_status: repeat_finding
remediation_note: |
  This is the meta-case in the registry: a report on whether other audits get acted on. The remediation status is "repeat_finding" because the underlying City Auditor recommendations themselves are the recommendations being tracked, and 20% of them are formally closed without implementation. The 2023 and 2022 status reports show the same closure rate (17%) and consistent pending-vs-implemented breakdowns.

  Three structural observations:

  First, "closed (no further follow-up planned)" is not synonymous with "the problem was fixed." Two of the four closure criteria explicitly contemplate non-implementation: (3) the agency disagrees and won't implement, and (4) Council considered but did not adopt. The cumulative 20% closure rate over 17 years includes a substantial share of acknowledged non-implementations.

  Second, the SPD recommendations show how long unimplemented recommendations can persist. The 2015 public-disclosure recommendation is now ten years old. The 2016 overtime recommendations and 2017 special-events recommendations are eight and seven years old respectively. SPD has been promising the Telestaff/UKG software procurement as the path to implementation in successive status reports. The audit was requested by then-Chief O'Toole; multiple chiefs have come and gone in the intervening decade.

  Third, the surveillance-technology reclassification mechanism is worth flagging. In 2024 the City CTO reclassified three Fire Department and City Light technologies as non-surveillance, which automatically closed 26 of the original 36 recommendations without implementation. The reclassification was permitted under Ordinance 125376 but functionally moots whatever issues the original audits raised. The City Auditor's report describes this matter-of-factly; it is, however, an oversight-system bypass mechanism.
reform_status: structural_change_failed
reform_status_note: |
  The City Auditor's annual status report is itself the structural reform - it makes implementation tracking visible to City Council and the public. The reform appears to be partially failing: the 17-23% closure-without-implementation rate has been consistent across the last three status reports (2023, 2024, 2025), suggesting an equilibrium rather than a trajectory toward implementation.

  No City Council action is taken on the closure rate as a structural matter. The City Auditor reports to Council; Council does not impose consequences for departmental non-implementation. The mechanism that would convert reports into action - departmental accountability for closing audit findings - is absent.

  An Inspector General regime (such as OIG-PSCSC for SPD) shifts the audit jurisdiction but does not change the underlying problem: independent auditors can recommend, but only legislative or executive action can compel.

# === REFORM ARGUMENT ===
reform_argument: |
  This case is the registry's load-bearing meta-case. Everywhere else in the registry there are audit findings, IG reports, settlements, and ombuds memos that describe what went wrong. This report describes what happens to the recommendations after the audits are written. The answer is: 72% get implemented (sometimes after years), 8% sit pending, and 20% get closed without being done.

  Three things make this report more interesting than it looks.

  One is the persistence of the SPD recommendations. The 2015 public-disclosure recommendation has survived three police chiefs, two mayoral administrations, and the entire arc of the Consent Decree. The 2016 overtime recommendations have been pending the same software procurement (Telestaff/UKG) across status reports for nine years. Reading these status entries year-over-year produces the impression that "we are working on procuring software" is a permanent steady-state response, not an interim status. If after ten years no software has been procured to address the underlying issue, the more parsimonious explanation is that the recommendation has been functionally rejected without the formal acknowledgment.

  Two is the surveillance-technology reclassification mechanism. In 2024, the Chief Technology Officer reclassified three technologies as non-surveillance, which automatically closed 26 recommendations from three prior audits. Ordinance 125376 permits the CTO to make this call. The City Auditor reports the closure neutrally. But the result is that 26 recommendations the City Auditor previously thought worth making were retroactively voided by an executive-branch reclassification decision. Whether the underlying privacy or operational concerns are gone is not addressed.

  Three is the structural problem the report inadvertently documents: an audit recommendation has no force absent a body willing to enforce it. The Office of City Auditor reports to City Council. City Council does not act on closures. Departments choose to implement or not. The system has no compulsion mechanism short of legislative action that almost never comes. This is the gap that an Inspector General regime with subpoena and reporting authority is designed to fill - which is exactly the structural argument running through several other cases in this registry (Cal Anderson SER, the SPD claims and lawsuits decade-review, the Disciplinary Determinations follow-up).

  The reform implication for centrist readers: the question is not whether Seattle has too few audits. It has plenty. The question is whether the audit-to-action conversion rate is acceptable for a city carrying open 2015-vintage recommendations on basic operational matters. Either the recommendations were never high-priority and the auditor's work product is being treated as advisory, or they were high-priority and the legislative branch lacks the will to enforce them. Both readings are damning in different directions.
