# === REFORM LINKAGE ===
reform_argument: |
  The statute already contains a for-cause standard: the governor "may remove
  the ombuds only for neglect of duty, misconduct, or the inability to perform
  duties" (RCW 43.06C.030). What it lacks is any process for establishing
  cause: no written findings, no hearing, no notice to the Legislature, and no
  review. Here the Governor's office cited misconduct, a listed ground, but the
  investigation into the underlying allegations was not completed or
  published before removal; the stated basis was refusal to follow the
  home-assignment order. Bourgeois says the removal was retaliation.

  Without a process, the public cannot tell a legitimate for-cause removal
  from a retaliatory one, and both sides' accounts stay unresolved. A
  procedural fix (written findings, notice to the Legislature's oversight
  committees, and an opportunity to respond) would protect the office's
  independence without weakening the Governor's ability to remove a director
  for real misconduct. See [reform: independent_inspector_general].

**Governor Bob Ferguson removed Jeremiah Bourgeois as director of the Office of the Corrections Ombuds (OCO) on April 24, 2026, citing insubordination and misconduct during an ethics investigation. Bourgeois denies the allegations and says he was removed for asserting the office's independence. The record supports neither account conclusively.**

## What happened

Ferguson appointed Bourgeois as OCO director in late 2025; he took office November 15, 2025 ([The Seattle Times](https://www.seattletimes.com/seattle-news/politics/wa-prison-watchdog-put-on-leave-accuses-governors-office-of-retaliation/)). Bourgeois served 27 years in prison, was released in 2019, graduated from Gonzaga Law School, and clerked for Court of Appeals Judge George Fearing ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2026/may/1/washington-governor-fires-independent-prison-watchdog/)).

In an April 20, 2026 letter, the Governor's chief operating officer, Franklin Plaistowe, placed Bourgeois on paid home assignment during an investigation and ordered him to stop work and avoid contact with staff. According to the letter as reported by The Seattle Times, the allegations included misuse of a prison ID badge and a state vehicle, awarding a contract to a friend kept under $10,000 to avoid review, and a "toxic" workplace with retaliation against employees who raised concerns. The letter said four employees had resigned and three had taken protected medical leave since Bourgeois took office ([The Seattle Times](https://www.seattletimes.com/seattle-news/politics/wa-prison-watchdog-put-on-leave-accuses-governors-office-of-retaliation/)).

Bourgeois refused the restrictions and posted on the office's official LinkedIn account that his efforts to make the OCO less beholden to the Department of Corrections had "apparently displeased" the Governor's office. On April 24, Plaistowe's termination letter cited those posts and an email to a staff member: "Your actions constitute direct insubordination and misconduct. Given the severity of this matter, the Governor is removing you" ([The Chronicle / Seattle Times](https://www.chronline.com/stories/gov-ferguson-fires-washington-prison-watchdog-citing-insubordination,401064)).

## The two accounts

**The Governor's office:** "Numerous concerns were raised by employees regarding problematic behavior by Mr. Bourgeois, resulting in formal complaints to Human Resources. ... When Mr. Bourgeois made it clear he refused to comply with home assignment and would continue to interact with staff, we were forced to separate Mr. Bourgeois" ([Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/former-juvenile-lifer-turned-prison-004541983.html)).

**Bourgeois:** He disputes the allegations and says the Governor's office blocked or delayed his hiring decisions, including bringing on retired Judge Fearing. "It was as if I was a director in name only," he said in August. "I pushed too hard, too fast. But ultimately, I did my job" ([Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/former-juvenile-lifer-turned-prison-004541983.html)).

## What the law says

The ombuds serves a three-year term, and "the governor may remove the ombuds only for neglect of duty, misconduct, or the inability to perform duties" ([RCW 43.06C.030](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=43.06C.030)). The statute does not say how cause is to be established, and it requires no findings, hearing, or notice to the Legislature.

## Status

Updated October 20, 2026. Heather Bates is acting director ([OCO](https://oco.wa.gov/staff/)), and the director position has been posted ([Prison Oversight](https://prisonoversight.org/developments/)). No legal challenge to the removal has been reported, and the findings of the underlying misconduct investigation have not been published.

An earlier version of this record said the statute did not specify grounds for removal, and its title linked the firing to the U.S. Department of Justice investigation of the Washington Corrections Center for Women, which opened 25 days later. The statute does set grounds, and no source connects the two events. See the [corrections log](/corrections).

## Why it's in the registry

It is the removal of the head of an independent oversight office, with the grounds disputed and no published findings.

## Reform implication

The fix is procedural, not a new for-cause standard, since the statute already has one. See [reform: independent_inspector_general].
