# === SCALE ===
dollars_at_issue: 3900000
dollars_basis: |
  OIG estimates the City spends approximately $3 million per year on sick leave paid to SPD officers who have functionally retired (extended leave status preceding formal separation), plus approximately $900,000 per year in continuing benefit accruals (retirement contributions and other employer-side benefits) that accrue while these officers are on leave but not working.

  OIG estimated that if the City instead paid 100% of accrued sick leave as a separation cash-out rather than allowing officers to ride out the balance on the active payroll, "the City would have saved an average of at least $1.7 million per year."

  Current sick-leave cash-out rate at separation: 25% (parity with most public-sector employers).
  Current vacation cash-out rate at separation: 100%.

  This 25%/100% asymmetry is the gaming mechanism: officers maximize value by burning sick leave (which would otherwise be paid out at 25%) while preserving vacation time (cashed out at 100%).
dollars_confirmed_loss: 3900000

# === STATUS ===
legal_status: audit_finding
outcome_summary: |
  Audit published January 22, 2026. OIG analyzed sworn SPD retirees over the period January 2018 to May 2024.

  Headline findings:
  - Nearly two-thirds of SPD officers who retired in the review window did so after going on extended leave (more than two weeks) preceding their formal separation
  - After 2020, the share of retiring officers using extended sick leave before retirement rose to 72%
  - Among officers retiring from extended leave status, the median tenure on the "unavailable" assignment list was 226 days
  - Of the 276 sworn employees who retired from extended leave status during the review window, 219 (79%) used every full day of accrued sick leave
  - The median SPD retiree cashed out 251 hours of vacation (~6 weeks) and less than 4 hours of sick leave - consistent with strategic depletion of sick balance before separation
  - Estimated annual City cost: $3M in sick-leave pay for "functionally retired" officers, plus ~$900K in continuing employer benefit accruals during these leave periods

  Mechanism: SPD policy nominally requires medical verification for extended sick leave, but officers who do not return to active duty never have to substantiate the verification. The current SPOG collective bargaining agreement contained no enforcement mechanism for verification of pre-retirement leave.

  SPD response, per the audit: the latest SPOG collective bargaining agreement includes a commitment to develop and implement a policy requiring health-care-provider verification of extended sick leave. SPD reported this policy would be implemented during 2026.

# === RESOLUTION TRACKING ===
remediation_status: in_progress
remediation_note: |
  The latest SPOG contract includes a negotiated commitment for SPD to develop a verification policy requiring health-care-provider documentation for extended sick leave. SPD's audit response stated this policy would be implemented during 2026. No published policy text or implementation timeline has been verified as of last verification.

  Three structural caveats on the remediation:

  First, the remediation requires a policy that does not yet exist. The contract creates an obligation to develop the policy; it does not itself contain the policy. The actual operational rule will be developed by SPD with SPOG input under the negotiated framework, which historically has produced longer implementation timelines and weaker rules than the original audit recommendations.

  Second, the 25%-versus-100% cash-out asymmetry that creates the gaming incentive was not addressed. Even with perfect medical verification, an officer who has 1,000 hours of accrued sick leave is still financially better off using those hours on the clock at 100% pay rather than cashing them out at 25% on separation. The asymmetry is a structural feature of the compensation package, not an enforcement problem. OIG does not propose changing the cash-out rate.

  Third, the audit covers only sworn SPD personnel. Whether similar patterns exist in other City agencies (Fire, Parks, Light, Utilities) was outside scope. The same cash-out asymmetry exists across the City compensation structure.

# === REFORM ARGUMENT ===
reform_argument: |
  This is the rare audit in this registry where the agency more or less agreed to fix the thing - and even so it is worth holding in the registry, because the fix being implemented is narrower than the problem being documented.

  Three things to keep in mind.

  One: this is the bluntest form of public-resources gaming in the SPD compensation system, and OIG had to wait for SPD to procure a new payroll system before it could even run the audit. The 2024 OIG Work Plan stated the project was "put on hold in 2024 due to staffing constraints at SPD and delays created by implementation of a new payroll system." That implementation delay means the audit period ends in May 2024, and the data needed to recompute the $3M/year estimate for the post-2020 period has been functionally locked behind agency IT decisions. The pattern this audit catches is years old; the audit landed in early 2026.

  Two: the verification policy is the easy fix, not the structural fix. Even with airtight medical verification, an officer with a large sick-leave balance has a financial incentive to use those hours on the clock rather than cash them out at 25%. The structural fix would be to equalize the sick-leave and vacation cash-out rates, or to apply the verification rule retroactively across the leave balance rather than to each individual extended-leave request. OIG does not propose either. The audit response gets credit for committing to the easy fix.

  Three: this is the type of finding that produces uncomfortable trade-offs even for centrist analysts. SPD is short on officers. Veteran officers nearing retirement are the marginal departures the department most wants to retain. The sick-leave-burn practice is, in functional terms, a retention bonus paid to departing officers. Tightening verification produces direct savings ($3M/year) but at the cost of a benefit that the workforce has come to expect and that has plausibly been baked into hiring and retention assumptions. The audit does not address the retention tradeoff explicitly; the SPOG contract addressed it by negotiating a future verification policy while presumably leaving other compensation elements alone.

  Where this case sits in the registry: it complements the other SPD cases (Disciplinary Determinations, Claims and Lawsuits decade-review, Cal Anderson SER) by showing a different severity type. The disciplinary cases are about insufficient accountability for misconduct. This case is about a compensation-system gaming pattern that costs the City millions of dollars per year and was not addressed for years because the audit took years to launch. Both patterns trace back to the same structural feature: an independent IG can document patterns but cannot compel timely fixes absent agency cooperation.
